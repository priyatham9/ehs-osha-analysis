# Which industries and years carry the ITA filing-quality problem

Generated by `python3 -m ehs_osha.industry_quality` from committed tables in `outputs/tables/`. Do not edit by hand: `tests/test_industry_quality.py` fails if this file differs from the script output.

## Question

OSHA publishes establishment-level Form 300A summaries collected through the Injury Tracking Application (ITA) and uses them to choose establishments for inspection. The repository's plausibility screen flags 57,857 of 2,801,064 filings (2.07%) for internally implausible values, and removing them moves the pooled aggregate TRIR from 0.134 to 3.983 (29.71x). This note asks where that problem sits:

1. Which NAICS sectors have the highest share of flagged filings, and is the difference from the pooled rate larger than sampling noise?
2. In which sectors does screening change the aggregate injury rate the most, and is that change broad or driven by a handful of filings?
3. Does the problem cluster by establishment size?
4. Is it concentrated in particular years (for example 2019) or present every year?

## Data

Only committed pipeline outputs are used; no raw filings are read. The tables are `quality_overall.csv`, `quality_by_naics2.csv`, `quality_by_year.csv`, `quality_hours_concentration.csv`, `quality_top50_implausible_hours.csv` and `size_band_effects.csv`, all produced by `scripts/run_analysis.py` on the 2016-2024 ITA Form 300A files under the default screen (120 to 4,500 hours per average employee per year). A filing is "flagged" when the `implausible` column of the screen is true; see the README section on the plausibility screen for the flags.

Sector rows use the first two digits of the filed NAICS code. Rows with fewer than 1,000 filings, or whose two-digit code is not a NAICS sector, are set aside: 48 such codes holding 335 filings (15 flagged). Code 99 is kept as its own row because it is large (1,890 filings) but it is not a NAICS sector.

## Method

- **Problem rate**: flagged filings divided by all filings in the group. The 95% interval is the Wilson score interval on those two counts. A sector is marked "above" or "below" when its whole interval lies above or below the pooled rate.
- **Screening effect**: the committed ratio of the screened aggregate TRIR to the unscreened aggregate TRIR (both are 200,000 x cases / hours, summed over filings).
- **Is the effect a few filings?** For each sector, the 50 flagged filings with the most reported hours (`quality_top50_implausible_hours.csv`) are removed from the unscreened hours and cases, and the unscreened TRIR and ratio are recomputed. This is the only filing-level detail committed, so it is a partial decomposition: the other flagged filings stay in.
- **Years**: Wilson intervals per year, and a Pearson chi-square test that the flagged share is the same in every year (2 x 9 table, closed-form p-value).
- No resampling is used, so no random seed is involved. Ratios and hour shares are reported without intervals because their uncertainty is dominated by single filings, which a binomial interval does not describe.

Construction (23) has the largest correction, 301.87x: its unscreened TRIR is 0.007 and its screened TRIR is 2.198. 2 of the top-50 filings are in construction and hold 99.5% of the sector's reported hours; without them the unscreened TRIR is 1.593 and the ratio falls to 1.38x. Manufacturing (33) is second at 11.89x, falling to 1.29x without its 5 top-50 filings. After removing the top-50 filings, every NAICS sector's ratio lies between 1.04x and 2.02x. The two rankings do not agree. Construction is below the pooled rate on problem rate (1.78%) and first on screening effect; real estate and finance are near the top on problem rate with modest screening effects. Filing-quality risk and rate distortion are different quantities and should be monitored separately.

The flagged share is never below 1.56% (2018) or above 2.60% (2021). It is not constant: the chi-square statistic for equal shares is 1,759 on 8 degrees of freedom (p < 1e-300). Every year from 2021 to 2024 has a higher share than every year from 2017 to 2019, with non-overlapping intervals. But it is present every year at a similar order of magnitude: the filing-level problem is persistent.

The rate distortion is concentrated. In 2019 flagged filings hold 99.6% of reported hours and the ratio is 249.28x; in 2024 it is 92.4% and 12.99x. In the other seven years the ratio stays between 1.39x and 2.28x, while 2019's flagged share (1.60%) is below the pooled rate. Pooled over all years, the single largest-hours filing holds 88.8% of all reported hours and the largest 10,000 hold 97.0% of hours but 8.7% of cases (`quality_hours_concentration.csv`).

## What it means

### For OSHA targeting

OSHA requires covered establishments to submit Form 300A data electronically each year under 29 CFR 1904.41 (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/1904.41, https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1904/1904.41) and publishes the submissions as ITA data (https://www.osha.gov/Establishment-Specific-Injury-and-Illness-Data). Its Site-Specific Targeting program, directive CPL 02-01-067 (https://www.osha.gov/enforcement/directives/cpl-02-01-067), selects establishments for inspection using rates computed from these submissions.

- A rate computed from an implausible hours field is wrong in a known direction: inflated hours push the rate toward zero and can hide an establishment from a high-rate list, while deflated hours push it up. The sectors with the highest flagged shares above (53, 52, 56) are where the largest share of site-level rates rests on a field that failed the screen.
- Because the effect on aggregates comes from a few filings and the problem rate is persistent across years, a fixed hours-per-employee check at submission (or before ranking) would remove most of the aggregate distortion in any year and would flag between 1.56 and 2.60 percent of filings a year for follow-up.
- Sector rankings of the screening effect should not be read as sector rankings of reporting behaviour; they mainly record where single extreme filings fell.

### For comparison with BLS SOII

The BLS Survey of Occupational Injuries and Illnesses (https://www.bls.gov/iif/overview/soii-overview.htm) publishes industry incidence rates from about 200,000 employer reports a year and excludes the self-employed, private households, farms with 10 or fewer employees, and federal workers. ITA covers a different population (establishments meeting the 1904.41 size and industry criteria), so the two will not agree exactly in any case.

- Unscreened ITA aggregates are not usable for a SOII comparison in 2019 and 2024 (unscreened TRIR 0.016 and 0.280) and are lower than the screened figure in 9 of 9 years. Any ITA versus SOII comparison should use screened aggregates.
- In construction and manufacturing (33) the unscreened sector figures are off by an order of magnitude or more; those are the sectors where an unscreened ITA versus SOII comparison would be most misleading.
- This repository has not entered any SOII rate (`summary.json` records the BLS lookup as not fetched), so no numerical ITA versus SOII gap is reported here.

## Limitations

- Built only from committed aggregate tables. The size-band problem rate, sector x year cross-tabulations, and intervals for ratios all need the raw filings and are not computed.
- The top-50 decomposition removes only the 50 largest-hours flagged filings across all years; flagged filings below that cut stay in the "without them" columns.
- Wilson intervals treat filings as independent. Establishments file in several years and `docs/PANEL.md` shows flags persist within establishments, so the intervals are narrower than a clustered interval would be.
- Sector codes are as filed. Miscoded NAICS values are left in the code they were filed under, and the set-aside rows above are not reassigned.
- "Flagged" means implausible under the default bounds, not proven wrong. The README's sensitivity grid shows the flagged count moves with the bounds while the aggregate correction barely does.
