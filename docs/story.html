The denominator

A safety rate is a fraction. Almost every argument about it happens in the numerator. In 2.8 million OSHA filings, the bottom half of the fraction is where the damage is.

Every number here is read from a committed file in this repository, named under the figure it sits in. Press P for presenter mode, M for the site map.

Two point eight million filings

Nine years of the public OSHA Injury Tracking Application, whole. Two percent of the rows cannot be true, and they hold almost all of the hours.

2,801,064 establishment-year filings
2016 to 2024, after 4,703 duplicate rows dropped
Scene 01 / step 1

Every establishment that files, nine years of it

2,801,064 establishment-year filings, 2016 through 2024, after 4,703 duplicate rows were dropped. Each dot here stands for about 2,800 of them. This is the whole public Injury Tracking Application panel, not a sample.

Scene 01 / step 2

57,857 of them cannot be true

The screen is arithmetic, not judgement: hours worked divided by average employees has to land between 120 and 4,500 a year, hours and employees have to be present, counts cannot be negative. 2.07% of filings fail. A rounding error, by count.

Scene 01 / step 3

They carry 96.7% of the hours

Those same dots hold 96.68% of every hour reported in the panel. One failure mode does it: 24,617 filings with hours per employee above the ceiling, 0.88% of filings, holding 96.65% of hours. Someone filed payroll hours for a company in the box meant for one site, or typed a year of hours nine times over.

What that does to the rate

Same filings, one screen applied or not. In 2019 the difference between the two answers is a factor of 249.

3.98 pooled screened TRIR
cases per 200,000 hours, 2016 to 2024
Scene 02 / step 1

Screened, the panel behaves

Aggregate TRIR on filings that pass the screen runs 3.15 to 4.60 across the nine years, pooled 3.98. Steady, plausible, the kind of series you would put on a slide.

Scene 02 / step 2

Unscreened, it falls through the floor

Take the same filings with no screen and the pooled rate is 0.13. The shaded gap is not an adjustment or a modelling choice. It is hours that do not exist inflating a denominator.

Scene 02 / step 3

2019: a factor of 249

In 2019, 99.60% of all reported hours sat in flagged filings. Unscreened TRIR reads 0.016; screened, 4.02. The correction factor is 249x. 2024 is the second worst at 13.0x. A year like that does not announce itself. It looks like a fantastic year.

Scene 02 / step 4

The window is not doing the work

The 120 to 4,500 bounds are a judgement call, so the pipeline sweeps 25 variants of them. Across the whole grid the corrected pooled TRIR moves only between 3.963 and 3.993, while the flagged share moves between 1.79% and 4.49%. Where you draw the line changes who gets excluded, not the answer.

Typos do not come back next year

Follow 1.4 million establishments from one filing year into the next and the flags turn out to be a property of the filer.

1,436,479 establishment-year pairs
each restricted to sites that filed in both years
Scene 03 / step 1

Two explanations, opposite predictions

Either implausible hours are independent keying errors, in which case being flagged this year says nothing about next year, or certain establishments misreport systematically and the flags cluster. 1,436,479 establishment-year pairs, restricted each time to establishments that filed in both years, can tell them apart.

Scene 03 / step 2

Flagged once, flagged again: 27.2%

Of 21,243 establishment-years that were flagged, 5,776 were flagged again the following year.

Scene 03 / step 3

Clean once: 0.95%

Of 1,415,236 clean establishment-years, 13,394 were flagged the next year. Odds ratio 39.1, Cohen's kappa 0.276, and the pattern holds in every one of the eight year pairs with odds ratios from 24.7 to 63.1. Kappa of 0.28 is the honest half of this: most flags in any year are still one-offs.

Against chance

Shuffle the flags 200 times and chance answers 1.01. The panel answers 39.1.

200 permutations, seed 20260909
flag counts held fixed within each year
Scene 04 / step 1

Reassign the flags at random, 200 times

Hold each year's number of flagged filings fixed and scatter those flags uniformly among that year's filers, year by year, with a fixed seed. Recompute the pooled persistence odds ratio each time. That is the typo hypothesis made explicit.

Scene 04 / step 2

Chance produces an odds ratio of one

The permutation mean is 1.012 with a 95% interval of 0.924 to 1.092. Narrow, and centred exactly where it should be if flags were independent year to year. The 200 individual draws are not stored in the repository, so this scene draws the interval the file reports, not a fabricated histogram.

Scene 04 / step 3

The observed value is 39.1

Zero of 200 permutations reached it. This is not a borderline result that needs a p-value convention to survive; it is a different order of magnitude. Bad hours are a property of certain filers, not a property of keyboards.

And the counts are not Poisson

Thirty large industries, four candidate models, one loser. Poisson never wins, and control limits are built on Poisson.

0 of 30 industries won by Poisson
30 largest three-digit NAICS groups, 2024 recordable counts
Scene 05 / step 1

Thirty large industries, four candidate models

Recordable counts for 2024 in the 30 largest three-digit NAICS groups with at least 500 screened establishments. Poisson, negative binomial, and the zero-inflated version of each, compared on AIC. Intercept-only, the split is 15 and 15 between NB2 and ZINB. Poisson never wins.

Scene 05 / step 2

Put the structure in the mean and look again

Add size-band and four-digit sub-industry fixed effects with a log-hours offset, and part of what looked like excess zeros turns out to have been mean heterogeneity: NB2 wins 20, ZINB 10. Overdispersion does not move. NB2 beats Poisson in all 30 industries at a boundary-corrected p below 0.001, with a median dispersion of 0.785.

Scene 05 / step 3

Why a VP should care about a distribution

Control limits, run charts and significance tests on injury counts are usually built on a Poisson assumption. On this panel that assumption is wrong in every large industry tested, and the limits it produces are too tight. Sites get flagged for a bad quarter that was ordinary variance.

06

What this does not establish

The screen excludes, it does not repair

Failing filings are dropped from aggregates because the true hours cannot be recovered from the filing alone. The persistence result suggests per-establishment repair might be possible; no repair has been attempted or validated.

The establishment key is a location, not a company

313,527 of 1,238,236 establishment IDs (25.3%) appear with more than one EIN across years, and 108,855 (8.8%) with more than one company name. State is stable for 99.7% of IDs. The panel result reads as a property of physical sites and their filing practice, not of corporate entities.

Nothing here is causal

Size-band and sub-industry coefficients are descriptive contrasts inside a screened administrative panel. Excess zeros cannot be split between incident-free operation and under-recording; the model measures the excess, not its source.

The BLS comparison was not made

The intended chart against the BLS SOII published rate is missing. The BLS series catalog returned HTTP 403 from this machine, so no series ID could be confirmed, and no published rate was typed in from memory. The attempt is logged in data/bls/PROVENANCE.txt.

Coverage is filers, not industry

The ITA panel covers establishments required to submit electronically: larger, and in higher-hazard industries. The 30 fitted NAICS groups were chosen on size alone and are a statement about large industries, not about every industry.

The day-count fields are unscreened

The screen acts on hours and employees only. Severity rates stay exposed to mistyped day totals; the swings in screened severity in 2022 and 2024 are a reason to build a day-count screen, not a finding about injuries.

07

Monday

Screen the hours before you quote the rate.

Take the establishment-year rows behind your last TRIR number. Divide hours worked by average employees. Anything outside 120 to 4,500 is not a data-quality footnote, it is a filing you cannot compute a rate from. Count how many there are, then count what share of your total hours they carry. If those two numbers disagree the way they do here, the rate you presented was about the bad rows.

Then check whether the same sites fail twice. If they do, the fix is a validation rule at entry and a conversation with whoever owns that site's payroll export, not an annual data cleanup.

Do it against this repository 
python scripts/download_data.py
python scripts/run_analysis.py
python3 -m ehs_osha.panel

Writes outputs/tables/quality_*.csv and panel_*.csv. The pipeline downloads the public files itself and fails loudly rather than substituting fixtures.
